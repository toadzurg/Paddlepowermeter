How this is calculated

The paddle swings about a pivot point on the shaft. Your top hand rises or drops so the blade stays buried to the throat through the drive, as good technique does, so strokes differ by direction and placement rather than by blade depth.

Water pushes on the blade face with drag that grows with the square of the blade's speed through the water. The boat settles where the average forward push equals hull drag. The hull is a C1 marathon canoe, calibrated from Shawn Burke's coast-down measurement of a USCA C-1 (The Science of Paddling, Part 14) plus wave drag at race pace.

Pushing off the boat's centreline turns it: paddling on the right turns the bow left. You correct by switching sides when the boat is off by the set heading; each switch loses part of a stroke. A stroke that turns the boat more means more switches.

Sideways push slows you too: the hull resists it by crabbing slightly, like a keel, which creates drag. The push comes in pulses, so the boat surges; drag grows with the square of speed, so uneven speed costs more, and more at slower rates.

Up and down push: a blade angled forward at the catch lifts the boat; angled back at the exit it pulls the boat down. The bent shaft shifts that balance toward the exit. The average changes how deep the boat sits, and the swing bounces it, making waves.

Race conditions: air drag changes with the air speed past you; a crosswind's side push makes the hull crab and turns the bow into the wind, so switching becomes lopsided; current adds to your speed over the ground; shallow water slows the boat's own wave so it drags harder (Burke, Part 4).

This is a simplified model for comparing techniques. It doesn't model waves, the blade's lift, or your body moving in the boat. Absolute speeds are approximate; differences between strokes are the useful part.
